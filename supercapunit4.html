UNIT 4 • DEVICE DESIGN

Geometry is a design choice

You know how charge is stored and what the electrodes are made of. Now: what shape should they be?

📐

In a micro supercapacitor the footprint is fixed by the device it powers, so performance has to come from how the electrodes are arranged — their height, width, spacing, and layout.

Key idea When you can't make the device bigger, you make the geometry smarter.

THE STARTING POINT

Planar interdigitated electrodes

+ − + − + − + −

Alternating positive and negative electrode fingers are patterned side by side on the same substrate, like two interlocking combs lying flat. This in-plane layout is well suited to tiny, on-chip devices.

No separate membrane is needed between stacked electrodes, and both electrodes come from the same lithography step.

THE LIMIT

Thin films hold little

Many planar micro supercapacitors are built from thin films or nanometer-scale coatings. That gives a large surface area per gram and short diffusion distances — but a very thin electrode contains only a small total amount of active material in the available footprint.

Per gram or volume

Can look excellent

Per unit area

Comes out low

Footprint is what's fixed, so areal capacitance is the number that matters.

THE FIX

Go vertical

🏙️

Instead of spreading out, extend the electrodes upward into tall, high-aspect-ratio fingers. The footprint stays the same, but the sidewalls add a much larger electrode-electrolyte interface.

More surface area Sidewalls add area for double-layer or faradaic storage
More active material Higher loading lifts areal capacitance and energy
Short ion path Lateral gaps stay small even as height grows

EVIDENCE

3D structures deliver

High-aspect-ratio silicon ≈ 90.7 mF/cm² areal capacitance
3D scaffold with sub-500 nm MnO₂ ≈ 10 µWh/cm² areal energy density
Alumina scaffold, 25 µm deep pores ≈ 137 mF/cm² at 2 mV/s, still ≈ 47 mF/cm² at 2000 mV/s
A thin active coating on a tall 3D scaffold gives big storage gains without a bigger footprint — and can keep good rate capability.

EVIDENCE IN CARBON

From 2.75 to 125

planar carbon ≤ 2.75 → 3D coated ≈ 125 mF/cm²

Planar pyrolytic-carbon devices were limited to about 2.75 mF/cm² or less because of how little carbon fits in a thin layer. Extending the electrode vertically and adding a pseudocapacitive coating reached about 125 mF/cm². Activated 3D carbon (C-MEMS) devices have approached about 75 mF/cm².

Height also boosts redox amplification: recall the jump from roughly 9× to 37× in taller carbon electrodes.

THE CATCH

Taller isn't always better

If electrodes become excessively thick, dense, or poorly wetted, ions can't penetrate the full depth during rapid cycling. That causes concentration gradients, higher ionic resistance, and incomplete use of the active material.

Capacitance retention at high scan rates can drop — the classic energy-power tradeoff.

COUPLED PARAMETERS

Six knobs, all linked

Electrode height

Finger width

Electrode spacing

Number of electrode pairs

Porosity

Aspect ratio

More height and more fingers raise active area; smaller gaps shorten ion paths. Change one and the others shift with it.

THE PRACTICAL LIMITS

Where designs break

Very narrow gaps Harder fabrication, poorer electrolyte infiltration, risk of shorting
Very tall electrodes Mechanically unstable and hard to reproduce
The best design isn't the most extreme one — it's the one that balances storage, transport, and what can actually be built.

FROM CONCEPT TO DEVICE

The four-layer stack

1. Glassy-carbon electrodes Interdigitated fingers from pyrolyzed SU-8
2. First polyimide Insulation the electrodes sit on, with openings to the electrodes and collectors
3. Titanium / silver Current collectors and contact pads
4. Second polyimide Final insulation, with openings over the pads
Fabrication starts from a 4-inch silicon oxide wafer cut into quarter circles, and the finished device is later stripped from it.

DESIGN REQUIREMENTS

What the design had to do

Big active area Interdigitated fingers packed into a small footprint
Tight gaps Small enough to raise capacitance, large enough to avoid shorts
Mechanical stability No curling, deformation, or fracture
Easy to test Pads sized for potentiostat clips
Gap size follows the same C = εA/d logic from Unit 1 — but the manufacturing limits set how far you can push it.

DESIGN REQUIREMENTS

Small enough to implant

Neural interface ≈ 24.26 mm diameter
Glucose monitor ≈ 27 × 24 × 4.7 mm
Pacemaker 20 mm × 4.5 mm
Test package A 20 mm coin-cell enclosure
These real device sizes — and the coin-cell package — cap how big the supercapacitor can be.

DESIGN 1 • DA VINCI

Maximum area, maximum trouble

🖌️

Da Vinci went for the most active area: 50 µm wide, 1.40 cm long fingers. But the device exceeded the 20 mm coin-cell space, the narrow long fingers cracked, delaminated, or broke, and undersized contact pads stressed the traces until devices failed during handling.

Never characterized — but it taught what to fix: size, finger fragility, and pad design.

DESIGN 2 • MEDICI

Smaller, but too tight

🏛️

Medici shrank to 0.40 cm fingers, fitting the coin cell and curling far less. But its tighter gaps hit the lithography limit: incomplete development of the negative resist left gaps too small or electrodes shorted together. The 50 µm fingers still broke, and the electrodes sat so close to the pads that electrolyte reached the silver.

Better packaging, but not enough yield or testability.

DESIGN 3 • MONA LISA

Wider fingers, new failure

🖼️

Mona Lisa widened the fingers to 100 µm (same length, 100 µm spacing). Lithography improved markedly, with fewer broken or underdeveloped fingers. But during pyrolysis, thermal stress made many fingers delaminate, and the electrodes were still too near the metal pads.

Good photolithography isn't enough — the device has to survive conversion to glassy carbon too.

DESIGN 4 • MARCO POLO

The benchmark

🧭

Marco Polo wasn't a new idea. It reused a previously validated design — large 750 µm × 2500 µm fingers, 200 µm spacing, 10 electrodes — as a dependable reference. It was fabricated at several glassy-carbon thicknesses with identical geometry, so thickness could be studied on its own.

Big electrodes were robust and consistent — the yardstick for judging the new compact geometry.

DESIGN 5 • GALILEO

The compromise that worked

🔭
Electrodes 25 interdigitated pairs, 75 µm wide, 100 µm gap, ≈ 3700 µm active length
Connection tabs Widened to 125 µm for stronger support at the collectors
Contact pads ≈ 3.5 × 4 mm, moved ≈ 2.5 mm from the electrodes
75 µm sits between Medici (50) and Mona Lisa (100): better yield than the first, less material than the second. Pads far from the electrodes keep silver out of the electrolyte.

BY THE NUMBERS

Five designs compared

Da Vinci 96 electrodes · 50 µm wide · 90 µm gap → 0.672 cm²
Medici 60 electrodes · 50 µm wide · 75 µm gap → 0.120 cm²
Mona Lisa 36 electrodes · 100 µm wide · 100 µm gap → 0.144 cm²
Marco Polo 10 electrodes · 750 µm wide · 200 µm gap → 0.1875 cm²
Galileo 50 electrodes · 75 µm wide · 100 µm gap → 0.139 cm²
Key idea Da Vinci had by far the most area and Medici the least — yet neither won. Yield, robustness, and testability beat raw surface area.
