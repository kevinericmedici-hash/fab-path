SCALING LAWS · PART 1 OF 3

Shrinking changes physics.

Engineers describe size with one number, S: the characteristic length of the device. Every other quantity is written as a power of S. If S shrinks by 10:

Length

[S1]

1 / 10

Area

[S2]

1 / 100

Volume

[S3]

1 / 1,000
Length, area and volume all shrink, but at different rates. That mismatch is the whole story of scaling laws.

WHY IT MATTERS

Your everyday intuition stops working.

A MEMS device is often about a thousandth the size of the big machine it replaces. At that size, effects you normally ignore take over, and effects you rely on fade away.

Garden hose Surface tension is irrelevant. You need a pump or gravity to move the water.
100 µm channel Surface tension can pull liquid along by itself. Paper test strips and some chips fill this way, with no pump at all.
Scaling laws are the new intuition: they tell you in advance which effects grow and which fade as a design shrinks.

SURFACE-TO-VOLUME RATIO

Small things are mostly surface.

Take a cube with side a. Its surface is 6a2 and its volume is a3, so

surface ÷ volume = 6a2 ÷ a3 = 6 / a  ∝  [S−1]
1 m cube 6 square metres of surface per cubic metre
1 mm cube 6,000 per metre: a thousand times more surface for its volume
10 µm cube 600,000 per metre
Anything that acts on a surface (friction, adhesion, surface tension, heat loss) grows in importance compared with anything that acts on the bulk.

LESSONS FROM NATURE

Animals already obey scaling laws.

Big land animals

Weight grows as S3 but bone strength only as S2, so an elephant needs thick legs. Gravity is the enemy.

Small creatures

A huge surface for their volume means they dry out fast, yet surface tension lets a water strider walk on a pond.

Sea animals

Buoyancy carries their weight, which is why the biggest animal ever, the blue whale, lives in water.

Large means fighting gravity; small means fighting surfaces. MEMS live firmly in the small world.

WHO WINS AT THE MICROSCALE

Compare each force with weight.

Weight follows volume, [S3]. Divide another force's scaling by S3 to see how it grows relative to weight as the device shrinks:

Surface tension [S1] S1 ÷ S3 = S−2. At 1,000× smaller it is a million times more important than weight.
Electrostatic [S2] S2 ÷ S3 = S−1. At 1,000× smaller it is a thousand times more important.
Weight [S3] The reference. It fades into the background.
This is why MEMS parts stick together (stiction) and why they are pushed around with electrostatic forces instead of weights and motors.
