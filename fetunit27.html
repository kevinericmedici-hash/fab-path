THE IMPERFECT SWITCH

Off is never quite off

Ideal switch Zero current when open, no matter what
A real transistor Always passes a trickle: the off current, Ioff
Why it matters A phone or server holds billions of transistors that sit idle most of the time
Three leaks Over the barrier, through the oxide, and across a junction
Switching energy is the visible cost of a chip. Leakage is the one that never sleeps.

OVER THE BARRIER

Subthreshold leakage

Ioff ≈ Ith × 10−Vth/S

Carriers in the source follow a Boltzmann distribution, so some always have enough energy to cross a barrier the gate has not fully raised. The current falls by a factor of 10 for every S of gate voltage below threshold, and at room temperature S cannot beat about 60 mV per decade. Lower Vth by 120 mV at S = 60 and the leakage is 100 times larger.

A faster, lower-threshold transistor leaks exponentially more.

THROUGH THE OXIDE

Gate tunneling

When the gate oxide is only a few atoms thick, electrons can tunnel straight through it. The leakage falls exponentially with thickness, and in the early 2000s SiO₂ was thin enough (about 1.2 nm) that the gate leakage of a whole chip became a serious fraction of its power. This is what drove the move to high-k dielectrics (Part 4).

Thin oxides give strong control, and the price is current through the gate.

THE BUDGET

Fast or quiet, pick a mix

Junction leakage A reverse-biased drain-to-body diode, and band-to-band tunneling at high doping, add a smaller trickle
Multi-Vth libraries Fast low-Vth cells on critical paths, slow high-Vth cells everywhere else
Power gating Switch off whole blocks that are idle, so they leak nothing
Temperature Leakage roughly doubles every 10 to 20 °C, so hot chips leak more
Chip designers spend threshold voltage like money: only where speed pays for the leakage.
