UNIT 5 • ELECTROLYTES

The invisible half of the device

Electrodes get the attention. The electrolyte decides how fast, how high, and how safely a supercapacitor runs.

💧

Between and inside the electrodes sits a liquid, gel, or solid full of mobile ions. Swap only that ingredient and the same electrodes can double their voltage, work in the cold, or stop leaking.

Key idea The electrolyte is not filler: it sets voltage, speed, temperature range, and safety.

THE JOB DESCRIPTION

Three jobs, one material

🧪

Ion source

Supplies the positive and negative ions that build the electric double layer

🚚

Ion highway

Carries ions between the electrodes, and deep into their pores

🛑

Electron blocker

Refuses to pass electrons, so charge can't short-circuit through the cell

Great ionic conduction, zero electronic conduction: that is the whole spec.

THE WISH LIST

What we'd want from an electrolyte

Wide voltage window Stays stable at high cell voltage, so energy grows
High ionic conductivity Ions move easily, so resistance stays low
Low viscosity, wide temperature range Still flows in the cold, still stable when hot
Chemical compatibility Doesn't attack electrodes, current collectors, or seals
Safety Non-flammable and low toxicity
Low cost Cheap, common, and easy to handle
No electrolyte wins every line. That is why several families exist.

WHAT'S IN THE BOTTLE

Salt, solvent, and solvation

salt + solvent → ions

Most electrolytes are a salt dissolved in a solvent. The salt splits into positive cations and negative anions, and solvent molecules cluster around each ion in a solvation shell. Ionic liquids skip the solvent, since the salt is already a liquid.

An ion in solution isn't bare: it arrives with a shell of solvent that changes how big it acts and how fast it moves.

CONDUCTIVITY MEETS POWER

Why resistance is the electrolyte's job too

P_max = V² / (4 · ESR)

For a matched load, a cell's maximum power grows with the square of its voltage and shrinks with its equivalent series resistance (ESR). The electrolyte's conductivity is a big part of ESR, and its window sets the voltage, so it steers power twice.

Voltage and resistance both live in the electrolyte.

THE VOLTAGE WINDOW

Why voltage is the prize

E = ½ C V²

Stored energy grows with the square of the voltage. Doubling the capacitance doubles the energy, but doubling the voltage quadruples it. So the voltage window an electrolyte allows is worth more than almost any other property.

Widen the window and energy climbs fast, which is why electrolytes are tied so closely to energy density.

WHAT LIMITS THE WINDOW

Where the electrolyte gives up

⬇️

Reduction limit

At the negative electrode, the ions or solvent start to gain electrons and break down

⬆️

Oxidation limit

At the positive electrode, they start to lose electrons and break down

↔️

The window

The gap between the two limits is the voltage range the cell can safely use

Past a limit, charge goes into destroying the electrolyte instead of storing energy.

READING THE EDGES

How to spot a window on a CV

Flat, box-shaped trace Charge is being stored capacitively, so the electrolyte is stable
Sharp current rise at either end Side reactions are breaking the electrolyte down
The cut-off matters Papers pick a current threshold, so a window depends on how it was defined
The electrode matters The same electrolyte can show different limits on carbon, metal, or oxide
A quoted window is a measurement with conditions attached, not a constant of nature.

PUSHING TOO FAR

What happens past the limit

Gas Gas forms and can swell a sealed cell
Resistance Breakdown products raise resistance and fade capacity
Leakage Side reactions raise leakage current and speed up self-discharge
Safety In the worst cases, heat and pressure build up
This is why cells are rated with margin below the limit: margin buys lifetime.

AQUEOUS ELECTROLYTES

Water-based: cheap, fast, forgiving

🍋

Acidic

H₂SO₄ solution. Very conductive, and common with carbon electrodes

🧼

Alkaline

KOH solution. Very conductive, and suited to many oxide electrodes

🧂

Neutral

Salts such as Na₂SO₄ or KCl. Milder and safer, with a wider window

Water dissolves salts well, needs no dry room, and doesn't burn.

WHY WATER IS FAST

Small ions, high conductivity

Conductivity Acids and bases often reach 0.4 S/cm or more, roughly ten times typical organic electrolytes
Protons hop H⁺ moves by passing between water molecules, which makes acids especially quick
Handling Assembled in ordinary air, with low cost and low fire risk
The catch Strong acids and bases attack many metal current collectors and seals
Low resistance and low cost make aqueous cells the workhorse of the lab.

THE 1.23 V CEILING

Water splits, and that caps the voltage

2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂

Thermodynamically, water splits into hydrogen and oxygen at about 1.23 V. Acidic and alkaline carbon cells usually run near 1 V. Neutral salt solutions can reach roughly 1.6 to 2 V, because gas evolution is slow on carbon and delays the splitting.

A 2.7 V organic cell holds about (2.7 / 1.0)² ≈ 7 times the energy of a 1 V cell with the same capacitance.

WATER-IN-SALT

Starving water of its freedom

salt ≫ water

In a water-in-salt electrolyte, so much salt is dissolved (around 20 moles per kilogram of water for some lithium salts) that nearly every water molecule is tied up around an ion. With little free water left to split, the window can stretch to roughly 2.5 to 3 V while staying non-flammable.

The catches: expensive salts and higher viscosity, so it trades some cost and speed for voltage.

ORGANIC ELECTROLYTES

Solvents that don't split

TEABF₄ in acetonitrile

Organic electrolytes dissolve a salt such as tetraethylammonium tetrafluoroborate (TEABF₄) in a solvent like acetonitrile or propylene carbonate. With no water to split, the window reaches about 2.5 to 2.7 V. This is the electrolyte in most commercial carbon supercapacitors.

More than double the voltage of water means several times the energy.

THE PRICE OF VOLTAGE

Organic: strengths and catches

Strengths

A wide window near 2.7 V, good low-temperature behavior, and a mature commercial supply chain

Catches

Lower conductivity than water, flammable and sometimes toxic solvents, and trace moisture that causes gas and aging, so cells need dry assembly and tight seals

Bulkier ions and lower conductivity also mean more resistance per cell than in water.

IONIC LIQUIDS

Salts that are liquid on their own

EMIM⁺ TFSI⁻: no solvent

An ionic liquid is a salt that stays molten at or near room temperature. Its bulky, lopsided ions (imidazolium or pyrrolidinium cations paired with anions such as BF₄⁻ or TFSI⁻) don't pack into a crystal easily. No solvent is needed: the liquid is the ions.

Ions with no solvent shell, at very high concentration.

WIDE WINDOW, THICK SYRUP

Ionic liquids: what you gain and give up

Voltage window Often 3 to 4 V, the widest of the liquids
Safety and stability Negligible vapor pressure, non-flammable, and stable at high temperature
Speed Viscous, with conductivity often around 10 mS/cm or lower at room temperature
Cold They thicken sharply as it cools, so power drops
Cost and ion size Expensive, and their large ions need large enough pores
A trade of speed and price for voltage and safety.

LIQUIDS COMPARED

Three liquid families at a glance

Aqueous About 1 V (up to about 3 V water-in-salt) · highest conductivity · cheapest · non-flammable
Organic About 2.5–2.7 V · moderate conductivity · flammable, needs dry assembly
Ionic liquid About 3–4 V · lowest conductivity · costly · non-flammable
Families can be mixed: an ionic liquid diluted in acetonitrile, for example, cuts viscosity while keeping a wide window.

GEL & SOLID-STATE

Take away the puddle

polymer + liquid → gel

Liquids leak, need a separator, and want a rigid case. A gel polymer electrolyte holds a liquid electrolyte inside a polymer network, so it acts like a soft solid while ions still move through it. A true solid electrolyte goes further and has no free liquid at all.

Gels trade some conductivity for leak-free, bendable devices.

RECIPES

Three families of gels and solids

💦

Hydrogels

A polymer such as PVA plus water and an acid, base, or salt (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄, KOH). The go-to for lab micro devices and wearables

🧈

Organogels & ionogels

A polymer such as PVDF-HFP holding an organic solvent or an ionic liquid, for a wider window

🧱

Solid polymers & inorganics

Ions hop along polymer chains (PEO plus a salt) or through ceramics and glasses. Safest, but slowest

The liquid inside a gel sets the window; the polymer sets the shape.

THE GEL AS SEPARATOR

The gel doubles as the separator

Fills the gap In in-plane and fiber devices, the gel sits between electrodes and keeps them apart
Leak-free and bendable No puddle to escape when a device flexes, folds, or stretches
Thin Lets the whole device stay slim and lightweight
Catch: conductivity Ions move slower than in a free liquid
Catch: pore contact A thick gel struggles to fill deep pores, so it is cast thin and runny, then allowed to set
For 3D micro-electrodes, getting the gel into the structure is a fabrication problem.

PAIRING WITH GEOMETRY

Match the electrolyte to the device shape

Coin cell or wound can Free liquid plus a porous separator, sealed tight
Pouch Liquid or gel, with foil sealing that keeps moisture out
In-plane micro device A gel, or a thin liquid layer held under a cover
Fiber and textile Gel between the fibers, so it flexes and doesn't drip
Stretchable film A stretchable hydrogel or ionogel
Implant or skin patch Neutral, biocompatible electrolytes such as body-like saline
Unit 4's shapes are only practical when the electrolyte can live inside them.

IONS HAVE SIZE

Ion size versus pore size

ion ≈ pore?

In water, an ion carries a shell of water molecules, so a small ion can act large. Pores have to be big enough for ions to enter, or the surface inside is wasted. In very small pores (under about a nanometer), ions can shed part of their shell and pack tightly, and capacitance per area can actually rise.

Choose the pores for the ions, or choose the ions for the pores.

TEMPERATURE

Hot, cold, and everything between

Aqueous Freezes near 0 °C (salt lowers it) and dries out when hot
Organic Typically about −40 °C to 65 °C, limited by volatility and flammability
Ionic liquid Stable when hot, but viscous and slow in the cold
Gel Hydrogels can dry out or freeze, so packaging must hold water in
Match the electrolyte to where the device will actually live.

REDOX-ACTIVE ELECTROLYTES

When the electrolyte stores charge too

I⁻ ⇄ I₃⁻

Some electrolytes carry a dissolved redox couple, such as iodide and triiodide, hydroquinone, or vanadium salts. The couple reacts quickly and reversibly at the electrode surface, adding pseudocapacitance from the liquid itself. It's the same redox idea behind the current amplification in Unit 2, used here for storage.

The catch: dissolved redox species can wander to the other electrode and discharge it, so self-discharge rises.

A DECISION GUIDE

Pick the electrolyte for the job

Cheap, safe, moderate energy Neutral or acidic aqueous
Pseudocapacitive oxides An aqueous electrolyte matched to the oxide, such as alkaline or neutral salts
Highest energy in a big cell Organic, around 2.7 V
Extreme heat or strict safety Ionic liquid
Flexible, thin, or wearable Gel electrolyte
Thinnest, safest, slowest Solid electrolyte
Key idea There is no best electrolyte. Choose the one whose window, speed, and packaging fit the device.
